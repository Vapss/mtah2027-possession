Team Possession from Incomplete Football Tracking:
A Last-Controlled-Ball Approach

More than a Hack 2027 — Phase 1: Ball Possession (Metrica Sports × FC Barcelona) · Technical document

Team: {{TEAM}} · Repository: {{REPO}} · Submission SHA-256 {{SHA}} · {{DATE}}

Abstract. Inferring team possession from incomplete tracking requires continuity between observations without treating every nearby player as the possessor. We assign a possession, dead-ball or abstention label to each of the 199,319 video frames. Candidate controls require an observed ball inside the pitch, speed no greater than 5 m/s and a player within 1.5 m with no rival as close. Possession is carried between controls and retained during duels. Stoppages combine organiser-supplied Smart Tagging with a logistic classifier trained on seven open DFL matches; restart rules establish the subsequent team state. External experiments support the stoppage component, but do not evaluate the complete submitted pipeline under the challenge's possession definition. Public-window scores are diagnostic rather than independent validation: earlier selections used that window, and we subsequently revised the selection process following the organisers' clarification. Missing ball observations and uncertain control timing remain the principal limitations. The deterministic numpy/pandas pipeline rebuilds the submitted file byte for byte in about 18 s.

1. Task and evaluation objective

Each frame gets H (home in possession), A (away), D (dead ball) or X (no answer). The ranking metric, spellF1, matches whole possessions (maximal H/A runs) one to one with the ground truth by temporal IoU, averaged over thresholds 0.3–0.7. macroF1 only breaks ties. Frequent team switches can fragment otherwise accurate predictions into possessions that do not match the reference intervals. We therefore prioritise possession structure over per-frame accuracy. The available ground-truth labels for this match cover a public 5-minute window: 17 possessions, with the ball detected in approximately 88% of its frames, compared with 59.3% across the two playing-half video intervals. These intervals include stoppages; coverage over the entire tactical recording is reported in Table 1.

The organisers' clarification restricts how this evidence may be used. The public window and diagnostics derived from it may check a fixed pipeline, but may not select parameters or components. Tuning requires external data with verified usage rights. The rules also prohibit additional human annotations of this match, comparison of predictions with the video, and transmission of provided data to hosted AI services. Organiser-supplied Smart Tagging is an input resource, distinct from annotations created by the team.

Our earlier selection process did use the public window. Section 5 explains the subsequent revision and the limits this history places on the reported scores; §9 describes compliance evidence. Decisions and their provenance are recorded in docs/DECISIONS.md, cited below as Dnn.

3. Method

3.1 Candidate control and possession continuity

The challenge defines a change of possession as the other team gaining controlled possession. While the ball is contested or loose, it stays with the last team. We operationalise this definition through candidate control frames and a carried team state. With dT(t) the distance from the ball to team T's nearest player and v(t) the ball's speed, frame t is a candidate control for team T when

ball seen in pitch∧dT≤1.5 m∧dT<dT‾∧v≤5 m/s.

These thresholds are physically motivated defaults, not a measured boundary between control and non-control (D29). The 1.5 m radius represents reach with allowance for tracking noise; 5 m/s represents a fast dribble, with 10 m/s or more used as a motivating example of pass speed rather than a universal lower bound. A one-frame dwell accepts control without an additional persistence requirement. These motivations do not independently validate the specific parameter combination; its provenance and unresolved justification are discussed in §5.1. Unknown speed, equal nearest-player distances and observations outside the pitch cannot establish a candidate control. Otherwise, the last accepted team state is carried within each half, subject to the duel and restart rules below. A change is dated to the rival's first accepted control frame (dwell = 1). Without an earlier forced restart, frames before the first control are X; the scorer back-fills leading abstentions within its evaluation intervals.

3.2 The duel rule

A rival candidate control changes possession only when the team currently in possession has nobody within 1.5 m of the ball. We treat simultaneous proximity as a duel and retain the current state (Fig. 2b). This implements our interpretation of the challenge's definition: "in phases where the ball is contested or loose, possession stays with the last team that had it". Our rule reuses the control radius rather than introducing a separate duel threshold (D34, D45).

3.3 Dead ball: tagged stoppages ∪ player-based stoppages

Tagged stoppages (D30). Each tagged restart closes a stoppage. The stoppage opens at the last episode of the ball seen outside the pitch in the preceding 40 s (120 s after a goal). Otherwise it covers the tag window only. Short episodes count: 69% of the stoppages opened by an episode shorter than 1 s end in a throw-in, corner or goal kick. This supports retaining short episodes, although the restart type does not verify each observed exit or its timing (D45, test B).

Player-based stoppages (D36, D45). Stoppages often reduce collective player speed and produce characteristic restart formations. A ridge logistic regression uses 29 order-invariant features to identify this pattern. They describe the median and 90th-percentile player speed over windows from ±1 s to 30 s before and after the frame, proximity to a corner, counts of players near touchlines and in the boxes, and spatial spread. The classifier uses neither ball observations nor player identities. Aggregation removes dependence on slot order, although identity swaps can still distort displacement-based speeds; the implementation filters implausible speeds before aggregation.

The model was trained only on the seven DFL matches, with standardised features and missing values replaced by training means. Its weights are embedded in the code. Decoder settings were selected on DFL with this match's missing-ball pattern overlaid: probability above 0.5, live gaps under 1 s closed, and dead runs under 4 s dropped. These temporal features use future as well as past observations, so inference is offline. Leave-one-match-out evaluation on DFL reports precision / recall / accuracy of 0.885 / 0.884 / 0.895. Lang et al. (2022) report 92% accuracy with player identities, but the different experimental settings preclude a direct performance comparison. Where fewer than 8 players have usable speed estimates, the detector withholds raw dead-frame evidence; temporal gap closing may still bridge such frames. Missing players alone do not establish a stoppage.

3.4 Restarts

The challenge defines a restart as having a clear possessor; our task is to infer that team and the restart time. At a tagged restart with a known team, the decoder forces the corresponding state unless an accepted, non-duel control has already occurred in the tag window before the estimated restart frame. Observed control thus takes precedence over the time estimate (D30, D32, D35); 17 restarts are forced this way. Conflicting team tags on the same frame produce no forced state.

For a stoppage without a closing restart tag, the decoder finds the first subsequent accepted control and assigns its team retrospectively from the first live frame. It does so only within the same half and without crossing another stoppage (D45); this rule applies 37 times in the match. The team assignment therefore uses later evidence rather than claiming that control was observed at the inferred restart.

4. Handling missing and noisy tracking

The ball is absent in approximately 41% of frames across the two playing-half video intervals. Ball presence falls to 19.9–36.9% in the least-observed analysis blocks, which last at most five minutes. Ball absence supplies no new control evidence, and speed is not computed across a gap. Carrying the previous state preserves continuity but can miss an unobserved change of team, especially during a long gap. Rejecting ambiguous control observations reduces unsupported switches without ensuring that the retained state is correct.

Player-based stoppage detection and tagged restarts provide evidence that does not require the ball to be observed. The 22 players are tracked more consistently than the ball, although missing players and slot swaps also affect this evidence. We explored gap filling from formation, self-supervised imputation, Smart Tagging and video detection. These alternatives were excluded for different reasons, including weak agreement with auxiliary tags and restrictions on the evidence available for selection (§6).

5. Selection protocol and evaluation evidence

5.1 Revised selection protocol

The research protocols recorded candidate variants, acceptance criteria and a preference order before their comparisons. However, earlier criteria explicitly used the public window and a gap-pattern benchmark that transplants missing-ball patterns onto that same window. Both depend on the public ground truth. Following the organisers' clarification, we restricted them to diagnostics of a fixed pipeline (D44). A separate read-only review by a coding agent traced the provenance of constants and component decisions (D45); this was a provenance audit, not external validation.

We re-applied the written selection rules after removing criteria based on ground-truth scores or quantities derived from the window, such as its 3.4 possessions per minute. The revised process admitted the following forms of support:

  • the challenge's own definition of possession, the laws of the game, physics or the literature;
  • external data whose rights are verified: DFL/IDSSE (CC-BY 4.0);
  • checks without public ground-truth labels: block structure and agreement with Smart Tagging phases.

This review changed the player-based stoppage component and retained the remaining rules on the grounds summarised in Table 3. The process revises the basis for selection; it does not erase the team's prior exposure to public scores.

The DFL results in Table 3 evaluate stoppage variants within the external benchmark's control-and-carry engine. That benchmark has neither the supplied Smart Tagging restarts nor the submitted duel rule, and DFL possession labels follow a different definition. These are component-level comparisons, not an external score for the complete submission. Leave-one-match-out fitting separates each evaluated match from classifier training, but decoder settings were selected using DFL results; the reported performance is not a separate held-out assessment of all design choices.

The submitted pipeline also satisfies the DFL-derived structural limits. Dead ball covers 48.2% of the halves, below the ceiling of 51.7%; the block with the highest dead-ball share reaches 70.8%, below 88.6%, and no block is left without possessions. The pipeline produces 3.11 possessions per minute, while agreement with Smart Tagging increases from 77.1% to 77.7%. These checks establish structural plausibility and auxiliary agreement, not prediction accuracy.

Prior exposure and unresolved justification. We knew before this review that the logistic variants scored higher on the public window. The documented basis for switching to W3 is removal of the ground-truth criterion followed by the recorded preference order, but this prior exposure prevents us from treating W3's public score as independent evidence. D45 also records public-ground-truth diagnostics and exploratory configurations before the control defaults were fixed. The unchanged 1.5 m radius, 5 m/s speed threshold and one-frame dwell retain their documented physical and decoding motivations, but subsequent external experiments used these defaults rather than independently validating the specific combination. Independent validation of this combination and the interpretation of compliance remain unresolved; revising the selection criteria does not by itself establish compliance.

The larger margin from correcting the team labels suggests that live-play assignment deserves attention. Further diagnostics implicate timing near touches and ball precision (D42, D43), but these substitutions do not isolate timing as the sole cause or rule out interactions with stoppage errors.

7. Limitations and further work

  • Timing near a touch. Estimated ball noise of about 1 m at low speed makes early-touch detections unreliable. The diagnostics suggest that control timing contributes to lost spellF1, but do not establish that it explains all remaining error. The tested timing rules provided insufficient admissible evidence for a change.
  • Dead-ball share. The pipeline labels 48% of the halves as D, within DFL's 41–52% per-match range. This is a plausibility check, not validation: the challenge may define stoppages more narrowly, and the public window cannot establish whether the full-match share is correct.
  • Long ball gaps. In the least-observed blocks (under 30% ball presence), the state is carried through gaps of over a minute. An unseen change can therefore propagate an incorrect team label. Better ball observations that transfer across halves are a plausible direction, but their benefit remains unmeasured.
  • External evaluation mismatch. DFL provides admissible component-level evidence, but its touch-based possession labels are finer than the challenge's control definition. Metrica data has no declared licence and supplies no selection evidence here. A licensed corpus aligned with the challenge's definition would allow a more appropriate assessment of possession changes.

The external training corpus is limited to seven matches, and transfer to this tracking export remains uncertain. Estimated tag-to-restart offsets introduce further timing uncertainty. Future observations in the stoppage features and retrospective restart assignment also make the current system unsuitable for causal, real-time inference without changes that would require separate evaluation.

8. Reproduction

The reported builds used Python 3.12.3. The labelling path depends only on numpy and pandas, with dependencies recorded in uv.lock. Inference requires no network access or training and uses no random sampling.

The commands below use a Unix-like shell. In Windows CMD, replace the export line with set "MTAH_DATA_DIR=C:\path\to\bundle".

uv sync --locked --no-dev --inexact                  # = make install
export MTAH_DATA_DIR=/path/to/bundle                 # licensed data, never in the repository
python tools/engine.py --build runs/engine --submission submissions/engine.csv   # = make engine
python evaluation/validate_submission.py submissions/engine.csv
python tools/repro.py --against submissions/engine.csv                           # = make repro

repro.py builds the pipeline twice from scratch and compares the output bytes; --against also checks them against the submitted file. The submitted file's SHA-256 is {{SHA}}. On an Intel i9-13980HX laptop (16 GB, Windows 11), one build takes 17.3–17.8 s and make repro takes 33.7 s. These are measured wall-clock times for that machine. The test B analysis, which does not use public ground-truth labels, is implemented in tools/readjudicate.py. External component experiments are implemented in tools/dead_ball_v2.py dfl-bench, using DFL data stored outside the repository; its directory argument and optional dropout mode are documented in that module.
